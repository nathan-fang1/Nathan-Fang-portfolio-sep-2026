How I work

It starts with being very good at the content.

Six years of making videos that went viral across very different formats. Everything else I do is built on that, and the work below is the proof. The higher rungs are how it scales past me.

LEVEL 01

Make it

Research what's already working, break down the patterns, pull out the elements that matter. Then draft, film, edit and post it myself. This is where the taste comes from.

LEVEL 02

Coach it

Teach creators to make it, in their own voice rather than mine. Making it myself taught me which small details actually move views: lighting, framing, hooks, delivery, scripts. Those are what I coach.

LEVEL 03

Systemize it

Turn the coaching into SOPs and repeatable formats, then train other coaches to run them. The knowledge stops living in my head.

LEVEL 04

Tool it

Build the tools that make the system easy to run: specs, dashboards, and performance data that surfaces on its own.

BeFreed · Growth & creator program · Feb–Aug 2026

120M views, and a program that kept running after I left

I didn't scale this program by doing more work myself. I scaled it by turning what I knew into systems other people could execute.

120.3MCampaign views
27Videos over 1M
6.2×Views per post
$0.53CPM, from $3.14

01 — What I walked into

A group of creators, not a system

I joined in February. There were 10–15 creators producing three or four videos a week, and a lot of those videos were doing about 1K views. The obvious read was that the creators needed to be better. The actual problem was that almost nothing around them was defined.

There was no warm-up process, so I spent time chasing creators to warm their accounts instead of having something repeatable that prepared them before they started producing. Sourcing leaned on a marketplace, which meant we sometimes ended up appeasing creators who were never going to be a fit. And the creators we did have weren't used to being coached hard — they came from a much looser UGC environment, and I was asking about lighting, framing, background, where they sat, how they delivered lines.

Some adapted. Some didn't. That sorting is where the coaching system started.

02 — The thing I got wrong first

Better execution wasn't enough

For two months I tried to make the existing strategy work better. Same BookTok-adjacent self-improvement content, cleaner. It didn't move.

Eventually the conclusion was uncomfortable: the creators weren't the ceiling. The creative premise was. You can't coach your way out of content that has no viral potential in it to begin with.

The strongest content wasn't selling “BeFreed has book summaries.” It was selling “I want to become this kind of person.”

03 — The May pivot

From book content to identity content

On May 1, I flew to San Francisco and pitched the team on a different creative direction: aspirational, visually distinctive, identity-driven. The format that carried it was a quadrant layout — images, aesthetic, subjects and language all signalling one specific kind of person.

The viewer is a college or post-grad person who wants to be intelligent, disciplined, cultured, interesting. They see the video and think that's who I want to be. BeFreed becomes the mechanism for getting there rather than the subject of the ad.

On May 12, Kate posted the first real proof: 1.2M views. I didn't know it was going to work. It was a hypothesis, and the market answered.

04 — The conversion mechanism

Push, then pull

One creator's script cracked the structure open. It sells the need to learn a set of concepts in order to become a different person, and only then does the product enter — not as an advertisement, but as the way the viewer personally consumes that knowledge.

Once it was written down as a mechanism instead of a lucky video, other creators could run it. 1.3M, then 1.1M from someone copying the format, then repeatedly across the roster. That's the difference between a viral video and an evergreen format.

05 — Turning it into leverage

Learn, diagnose, teach, document, delegate

Coaching capability

A coach who could replace me

One teammate had real content experience but had never coached anyone. I taught her how to give feedback, when to push and when to reassure, which lever to pull first — then wrote it all into a How to Coach SOP so it wasn't stuck in conversation.

Scripting

Stop flattening the inspiration

A scriptwriter was finding good references and then running them through ChatGPT, which sanded off everything that made them work. Rather than rewrite her scripts, I spent an hour teaching her how to modify an idea without diluting it.

Sourcing

Outreach that stopped getting ghosted

We read real email chains together and diagnosed the diction, what to reveal, what to emphasise, how to negotiate. Then I rewrote the templates and the SOP. Sourcing moved off the marketplace and onto the For You page.

Infrastructure

An internal creator CRM

Coaches kept asking the same three questions: who's performing, what's working, who's slipping. I specced a tool that pulled TikTok and Instagram data every four hours and stored view snapshots, and worked with an engineer to ship it in July.

06 — The real test

What was left behind

By August the program had a creator cohort, a sourcing process, onboarding, a coaching process, a scripting process, a compensation structure, a CRM, two proven viral formats, and coaches who could run all of it. My last job was teaching the people taking over.

The test was never whether the program could produce viral videos while I was there. It was whether it could keep producing them after I left.

A note on the numbers: these are program-level campaign benchmarks across two campaigns, not a controlled before/after experiment. The “before” window includes some later activity from creators who stayed on the old structure.

Turbo AI · UGC coaching & influencer marketing · Jul–Dec 2025

196M views, and learning to teach what I'd only ever done myself

I joined as a part-time UGC coach because I was a creator. The job turned out to be transferring creative judgment to other people.

196MOrganic UGC views, 6 months
50MViews in peak month
~100Creators coached
50Videos reviewed a day

01 — The job

Viral instinct, made teachable

Turbo AI is an AI study platform. It grew from about 4 million users when I joined to more than 11 million. The company's thesis was that people who had developed an instinct for what makes content travel could teach it to other creators, so I started part-time as a UGC coach, working with roughly 40 creators at a time inside an existing review-and-approval system.

The coaching went well enough that they brought me on full-time and handed me a second problem to solve: influencer marketing. I ran that lane on my own, from sourcing through payment.

02 — Lane one

Feedback someone can act on in the next take

At peak I was reviewing about 50 videos a day. “Be more energetic” or “improve your hook” doesn't tell anyone what to change, so I learned to break a video into the parts that weaken it: delivery and cadence, dead space between thoughts, caption timing and length, framing and light, whether the hook opened a question, and whether the product showed up as a list of features or as part of the creator's workflow.

Every note followed the same loop: name the specific problem, explain what it does to the viewer, give one concrete adjustment, review the next version. Sometimes the fix was as small as telling someone to explain it like they were on FaceTime with a friend.

03 — Emma

From no audience to a 50M-view video

Emma came into the program without an established audience or any real foundation in making content. Nothing was broken in an obvious way; she just didn't yet have the fundamentals that experienced creators take for granted.

We worked through them one at a time. Cutting the dead space so the video never stalled. Using captions as a second hook rather than a transcript. Getting her face into better light and making the background less dominant. Finding her natural speaking voice instead of one that sounded performed for the camera. Then we tested different creative angles and narrative approaches until one felt like her.

One of her Instagram videos reached more than 50 million views, and she grew from zero followers to over 70,000. Emma made and posted every video. What I contributed was seeing what was missing from her content specifically, explaining the fix in a way she could act on, and keeping the iterations going until it clicked.

04 — Managing people

Absorbing the mess so creators didn’t have to

Creator performance depended on the relationship as much as the brief. I took a real interest in the creators' lives, gave advice on their own accounts, and was direct when something wasn't working while praising what was. Creators who started out difficult often became easy to work with once there was trust.

Day-to-day communication moved to iMessage group chats, because a text gets answered faster than Discord or email. At one point I was running more than 100 of them. It was messy and hard to switch off from, but it meant less ghosting and faster turnaround on revisions.

Sometimes the organization needs to absorb more friction so that creators experience less of it.

05 — Lane two

An influencer program, and where reach stopped being enough

VAs sourced off TikTok and Instagram's own recommendations rather than hashtag tools, building a sheet that reached about 2,000 rows. I owned everything after that: calls, negotiation, contracts, creative review, verification and payment, with 30–50 influencers active at once. I asked for their rate first, anchored to a $2–3 CPM against average views, and offered bonuses such as $2,000 for a million views inside 14 days. Experienced creators often turned performance deals down.

Sebastian's script looked fine but dropped the open loops that made his own breakout video work; one round of specific feedback and the reel did 2.1M. Nikki's integration kept to two features inside her usual storytelling and reached 992K.

The program did about 50M organic views in four months and was discontinued on profitability and scale. We had no rigorous conversion tracking, so nobody could say which creators were paying back. My role ended when this lane didn't work out.

06 — What I took away

Three lessons I still use

Viral intuition only matters if you can explain it well enough for someone else to execute it. Creator performance is partly a management problem: clear expectations, fast feedback, and someone who follows through. And views are an input, not the outcome; a program has to be measured against what it costs and returns.

I also wrote the influencer work up as a five-stage guide (selection, sourcing, negotiation, management, post-campaign evaluation) for teams deciding whether the channel is worth running at all.

UGC figures cover the creators I personally coached and are separate from the influencer program's results.

Iru · Creative direction · Jul 2026 — present

From 6K average views to 6.8M+

The concept already existed. What changed was the process around it — and then the process kept going after the first viral videos.

6.8M+Views, three breakouts
~8×Follower growth
2MViews in two days, latest
6–7Flops before it worked

01 — Where Iru started

Trying a lot, with no direction

Iru is a social networking app for meeting new people and doing things together in real life. When I started in July 2026, two content interns, Piper and Amethyst, were producing constantly: street interviews, skits, memes.

Across roughly 17 videos in two weeks in June, the account averaged about 6K views, with plenty at 1–2K. A few cleared 10K, but those were usually short funny memes with nothing to do with Iru or the experience of using it. Followers weren't moving.

The problem wasn't effort. It was that nobody had defined what a good swing looked like, and the execution was often weak in ways that had nothing to do with the idea.

02 — First pass

The videos weren't good enough to watch

Faces weren't centred. Text sat too low and moved between videos, so the viewer's eyes had to hunt between cuts. Lighting was inconsistent, angles weren't chosen, pacing was slow, captions were messy, and the videos often didn't give you enough to understand what was happening.

I wrote filming and editing guides for the recurring problems — not film theory, just rules they could use while shooting and cutting on their phones. I never touched the footage. Piper and Amethyst were in New York and did all of the filming and editing. My job was their creative decisions.

Cleaner videos, though, are not the same as videos worth watching. The bigger question was what makes this particular kind of content work at all.

03 — Changing approach

I stopped fixing videos and went looking for answers

The team's best idea was a “take a stranger to lunch” video that had done almost 14K. The premise fit Iru perfectly — approach a stranger, start a conversation, end up doing something together is literally the behaviour the app exists to enable. But six or seven attempts landed between 1K and 4K.

I'd been giving reactive notes. Fix this shot. Make this faster. Improve the hook. Eventually I realised I was teaching them to patch their own videos instead of showing them how the format is actually built.

So I spent several hours on TikTok and Instagram and found 10–20 viral videos in the format across four or five accounts, then screenshared the research and broke them down with the interns. Not to copy a creator — to find the structure underneath.

What the working versions had in common

01

The hook states the premise. “I just moved to NYC and am trying to make friends — could we grab food?” You know the situation and the problem in one line.

02

Tension arrives immediately. The stranger doesn't say yes right away. That resistance is the reason to keep watching.

03

The camera feels accidental. Awkward low angles, alternating shots. It reads as secretly filming an experience, not producing an ad.

04

There's an implied storyline. Not “we asked people to be our friends” but new city, lonely, scared, will she say yes, will we get along.

05

It creates a specific want. The viewer is watching someone do the thing they wish they were brave enough to do.

06

The comedy serves the story. Jokes prove the two are getting along. They aren't random memes dropped in for retention.

04 — Adapting it

Why this format, for this product

We didn't copy a viral video. We asked why the format made sense for Iru, and the answer was unusually clean: meeting strangers, overcoming loneliness, finding people with a similar personality, doing something fun in New York. That is the product.

So we built the target viewer into the content. Someone who recently moved to the city and is struggling to build a social life. The hook said it out loud, and later in the conversation the creators steered toward loneliness and how hard it is to make friends somewhere new. Nobody is told here is an advertisement. They're shown the experience the app is for.

The biggest unlock: they didn't actually need to take a stranger to lunch. The stranger was part of the premise, not a requirement of the format.

05 — The breakthrough

1.1M, then 2.1M

Once Piper and Amethyst played both sides themselves, they had chemistry and control over the performance instead of hoping a real stranger would deliver. They leaned into the premise and faked the interaction.

Video one: 1.1M views, 106K likes, 150 comments, 1,738 reposts, 4,731 shares. Video two, same structure: 2.1M views, 220K likes, 416 comments, 3,461 reposts, 15.1K shares. 3.2M+ views between them, and the account went from around 1,000 followers to 3,627.

06 — Whether it reached the right people

Not just traffic

Across the two videos, 48% of the audience was 18–24, 42% in the US, 10% in New York. More telling was the comment section: “I wish it was this easy 😭”, “omg how it's hard to watch this as someone who has social anxiety”.

People weren't just entertained by the premise. They recognised themselves in the problem, which is the audience Iru is built for.

07 — The next problem

Reach wasn't turning into product action

The two viral videos worked as entertainment. They created a lot of desire around making friends in New York, and then didn't do enough with it. The team was saying the name Iru during the plug, but a name means nothing to someone who has never heard of the app.

Which left a specific question: how do you explain what a product actually does in seven to nine seconds without killing the video?

08 — Making the product visible

Same seven seconds, far more information

I showed Piper and Amethyst how we'd handled this on the BeFreed UGC campaign: screen recordings and screenshot overlays that let a viewer understand a product without anyone narrating a feature list. The plug didn't get longer. It stayed at seven to nine seconds and got denser.

While the creators described how Iru worked, we laid the exact visuals over the top — app screenshots, screen recordings, the logo, the specific UI they were referring to. I coached them on the execution, because the whole thing has to land inside a few seconds. The product stopped being a separate commercial and became a visual explanation inside the story.

Find the format, prove the format, find what the format isn't doing, fix that, run it again.

09 — The third iteration

2M views in two days

The next version of the format did 2M views in its first two days and has since reached 3.6M. Vedant says it's converting better than the first two. I don't have exact conversion numbers, so I won't put a percentage on it.

The account is at roughly 8K followers now, against about 1K when I started. Across the three breakouts the format has done 6.8M+ views.

10 — Honestly

What this was, and what it wasn't

I didn't shoot or edit anything. The interns produced all of it. What I contributed was the thinking around the content: diagnosing the weaknesses, coaching several times a week, researching what was already working, pulling the structure out of it, adapting that structure to Iru's audience, and then reading the results closely enough to find the next problem.

Three copies of the format did 31K, 27K and 10K. Not everything goes viral, and that's fine — the point was that Iru had a direction worth iterating on instead of a queue of unrelated experiments. I wrote a brief and had the creators record a walkthrough of the winning video so the next cohort could pick it up.

The change wasn't that I gave them better ideas. It was moving the team from “let's try this” to “here's why this format works, here's why it fits Iru, here's how it has to be executed, and here's what the last video told us about the next one.”

My own accounts · 2020—present

Six years of making the thing myself

Comedy taught me reach. An anonymous account taught me resonance. They're different problems.

400KFollowers, 5 accounts
8MBreakout video
800KFinance video, new niche
2 yrsSince I posted, still asked

01 — Comedy

400K followers before I understood why

Across two Instagram accounts, two TikTok accounts and Facebook, I built a combined following of about 400K, most of it making comedy. No deep audience relationship, no narrative arc, but a decent instinct for what made people send a video to someone else.

It taught me what pure distribution feels like, and that reach and resonance are separate problems. I got interested in the second one.

02 — The anonymous account

A breakup, in public, with no plan

After a bad breakup I started an anonymous TikTok to process it out loud. For months nothing landed. Then I found a format — minimally edited, no effects, just the thought — that could carry nuanced emotional content in a way I wasn't seeing elsewhere.

That became around 60K followers, 2M likes and an 8M view video. The number I actually care about is different: two years after I stopped, people still comment asking where I went, or telling me the videos helped them through their own breakup.

Going viral is a miracle. Every nuanced element has to land in one video, and it still has to say what you meant to say.

03 — And since

Documenting the awkward parts too

After graduating from UCSD I documented trying to break into an industry I had no credentials for. An unscripted rant about being unemployed did 400K views and a YouTube video crossed 100K. Useful practice in making an uncomfortable situation legible to strangers without making it alarming.

04 — The clearest example of the craft

800K views by counter-positioning into a new niche

I spent six years building humor content, including breakup videos that taught me how to tell stories, build emotional tension, and hold attention. When I entered finance, I noticed most creators relied on charts, indoor setups, and CNBC-style delivery.

So I brought the opposite: casual delivery, the storytelling instincts I'd developed through breakup content, and my existing comedic style. Instead of simply explaining the SpaceX IPO, I made a bold claim: it might be one of the biggest rug pulls in history. I layered in Vine booms and irreverent humor.

The result was an 800K-view video in a niche where I had no established track record. I didn't win by knowing more about stocks than Bloomberg. I won by communicating a topic people cared about in a way other finance creators couldn't easily replicate.

I kept running the same format. A month later, on the Leopold Aschenbrenner news, it did 800K again.

About

I'm a content person who ended up building systems

I've spent about six years making short-form video and studying why some of it travels. First on my own accounts, then inside startups, where the question stopped being “can I make a video that works” and became “can a team of people I don't control make videos that work, repeatedly, after I stop paying attention.”

Most of my work looks like diagnosis. A program isn't performing, and the cause is either the creative premise, the execution, the creators, or the system around them. They need very different fixes, and picking the wrong one wastes months. I've wasted a couple of months on the wrong one before, which is mostly how I learned to tell them apart.

I'm from California, studied at UCSD, and I'm currently taking on a small number of projects with startups who need organic distribution and don't have a budget for paid.
